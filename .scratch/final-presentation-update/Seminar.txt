NOTES: Before introducing the technical side of the project, I want to begin with a real historical example that demonstrates why maintenance scheduling is so important. | During the 1995 United States Army Blackhawk deployment to Bosnia, a battalion reported an overall readiness level of 89 percent. | However, when deployment orders were issued, the commander required each aircraft to have at least 75 residual flight hours remaining before deployment. | Because the battalion had not properly staggered its aircraft maintenance cycles, nine out of the 24 helicopters were instantly deemed non-deployable. | As a result, the battalion experienced a major reduction in combat power despite appearing ready on paper. | This example highlights a key issue in fleet maintenance scheduling: high average readiness does not necessarily translate into operational readiness if maintenance events are poorly distributed. | This is the main motivation behind my project, developing  | optimisation |  algorithms that create smoother and more balanced maintenance schedules to  | maximise |  long-term fleet availability. | 2

 Aircraft undergo scheduled maintenance events

NOTES: High variance in availability happens when short term flight allocation causes multiple aircraft to exhaust their Residual Flight Time concurrently. If they all suddenly fail, there will not be enough resources such as dock space, mechanics and components to fix them all. This is why we need to stagger and space the maintenance checks out. | However, even if we manage to space the schedules out, random failures can also happen which causes disruptions in whole schedule, leading to a cascading effect. | 3

Title 1: Literature Review and Method Selection
NOTES: In order to solve this problem efficiently, I conducted a literature review into existing aircraft fleet scheduling and maintenance  | optimisation |  techniques. The three major approaches were investigated: mathematical programming, stochastic simulation, and metaheuristic algorithms. | Mathematical programming methods can produce optimal solutions, but they often struggle with scalability and random failures. | Stochastic simulation is useful for modelling uncertainty and random failures, | However, metaheuristic algorithms are particularly effective for large-scale scheduling  | optimisation |  because they provide flexible and computationally efficient search strategies. | Based on these findings, I decided to pursue a hybrid  | optimisation |  approach using Simulated Annealing combined with Variable Neighbourhood Search. | The goal is to combine the global exploration capability of simulated annealing with the strong local search performance of variable neighborhood search. | 4

NOTES: To  | optimise |  the initial schedule, I implemented a Best-Improvement Neighbourhood Search algorithm. Since the dataset was extremely simple, advanced metaheuristic algorithms are not required yet and a simple neighborhood search can solve the problem. | The algorithm explores  | neighbouring |  solutions by shifting maintenance start dates while ensuring all scheduling constraints remain feasible, including the required 320 to 380 day maintenance intervals. | The  | optimisation |  objective was  | prioritised |  in two stages. First,  | maximise |  the minimum number of available aircraft across the schedule. Then, if multiple schedules achieved the same minimum availability, standard deviation was used as a secondary metric to smooth the schedule further. | As shown, neighborhood search successfully removed the major dips in availability and produced a much more balanced maintenance schedule. | This took about 300 iterations and only around a minute on my laptop to run. | 6

SLIDE 8
Title 1: Optimised Heavy maintenance dataset	
NOTES: After extending the dataset to include realistic A, B, C, and D maintenance checks, I developed a scheduling  | optimiser |  using a Variable Neighbourhood Search approach. | This flowchart  | summarises |  the  | optimisation |  process. | The algorithm first computes aircraft availability across the entire 12-year forecast horizon and evaluates the schedule using three metrics: minimum availability, standard deviation, and average availability. | It then generates  | neighbouring |  schedules by shifting maintenance start dates within their feasible maintenance windows. | Each candidate schedule undergoes a feasibility check to ensure there are no overlapping maintenance events. | Initially, I attempted to use the same brute-force  | neighbourhood |  search approach that worked on the smaller dataset. However, the problem became significantly larger after scaling from 10 aircraft to 24 aircraft, with around 11 to 12 maintenance events per aircraft. | Because the  | neighbourhood |  size is determined by aircraft multiplied by events multiplied by shift combinations, each iteration could generate over 1000  | neighbouring |  schedules. | Evaluating every  | neighbour |  became computationally expensive, and the algorithm was taking more than 30 minutes to complete. | To solve this issue, I introduced a random sampling strategy. Instead of evaluating all  | neighbours | , the  | optimiser |  randomly selects 200 candidate  | neighbours |  each iteration. | This significantly reduced runtime while still consistently finding improved schedules, because 200 sampled  | neighbours |  were sufficient to identify better local solutions. | The  | optimiser |  then iteratively explores different shift sizes and accepts improvements that produce smoother fleet availability. | This process repeats until no further improvements can be found. | 8
